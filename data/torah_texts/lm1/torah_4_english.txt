Torah 4 opens with "I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of Egypt" and teaches that when a person truly knows everything that happens is for his good, this awareness is itself a taste of the World to Come. The central thesis is the unification of divine attributes — the Tetragrammaton (mercy) and Elohim (judgment) both stem from love. This unified perception can only be achieved by elevating divine Sovereignty (Malkhut) from exile among the nations. The primary mechanism is verbal confession before a Torah scholar, which draws corrupt letter-combinations out from one's bones and reconstitutes them as holy speech. The Torah scholar's power to atone comes from his humility — true wisdom comes from "nothingness." The teaching presents three levels of connecting to the righteous: seeing the tzaddik (overcomes sadness and evil desires), giving charity (overcomes idle speech and pride), and verbal confession (enables proper soul-guidance). When a person confesses before a humble Torah scholar, one becomes included in the Ein Sof (Infinite). This involves "stripping away physicality" in a mode of "running and returning" — reaching ecstatic union with the Infinite and then returning to embodied consciousness, retaining the impression of unity. The teaching maps these concepts onto a Talmudic tale and interprets the four negative character traits as corresponding to the four elements: sadness (mineral), evil desires (vegetative), idle speech (animate), and pride (speaking). The opening verse is explained: "I am the Lord your God" means whether mercy or judgment, it is all one divine source; "who brought you out of Egypt" refers to liberation through the tzaddik elevating holy Sovereignty.
