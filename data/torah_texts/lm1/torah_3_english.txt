Torah 3 opens with the Talmudic tale of Rabbah bar bar Chana seeing a giant frog swallowed by a sea creature, swallowed by a raven perching on a tree. The central theme is the spiritual power and danger of music. Music from a wicked musician harms one's service of God, while music from a righteous musician is beneficial. The singing voice originates from birds — righteous musicians draw melody from two holy birds that nurse from the same source as prophets (which is why a cantor is called "chazan," from "chazon"/prophetic vision). Wicked musicians draw from impure birds that feed parasitically from the holy Malkhut. The remedy is studying the Oral Torah (Talmud) at night, which rectifies the six rings of the trachea from which the voice emerges. Even Torah study done with impure motivations receives protection through a "thread of lovingkindness" when performed at night. The Talmudic tale is decoded: the frog represents a bird of knowledge; sixty houses represent sixty tractates of the Talmud; the sea creature represents studying Torah for ulterior motives; the raven represents nighttime study; and the tree represents Abraham's lovingkindness. The teaching connects to kingship — David's musical ability demonstrated his fitness for sovereignty. Music, prophecy, and sovereignty are deeply intertwined. In the current exile, holy Malkhut is diminished, which is why cantors lack true understanding. In the messianic era, music will embody true wisdom. Practical takeaways: be discerning about whose music you listen to; study Talmud at night as a remedy; understand that even imperfect study receives divine protection at night; and know that the full restoration of holy music awaits the messianic era.
